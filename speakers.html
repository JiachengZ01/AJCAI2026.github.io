Talk title

Learning What Matters: Towards Dependable AI for Social Good

Abstract

The rapid advancement of foundation models has transformed the capabilities of artificial intelligence. Yet increasing model capability does not necessarily translate into reliable performance in the real world. From identifying individual animals in natural habitats to forecasting environmental extremes and understanding complex societal phenomena, AI systems must distinguish relevant information from distracting signals, generalise across unfamiliar contexts, and determine which knowledge to retain, transfer or revise. In this talk, I will explore these challenges through our recent research in multimodal learning, domain generalisation and continual learning. I will examine how fine-grained representations improve recognition, why large multimodal models struggle to identify relevant evidence, and how selective knowledge transfer and unlearning can improve model generalisation and adaptation. Drawing on research in biodiversity conservation and environmental intelligence, I will illustrate how real-world problems motivate fundamental advances in machine learning. I will conclude by discussing a research agenda for dependable AI for social good, where progress is measured not only by what AI systems are capable of learning, but also by how effectively they identify, integrate and use knowledge in the contexts where it matters.

Biography

Professor Yun Sing Koh is a Professor in the School of Computer Science at the University of Auckland, New Zealand, where she is Director of the Centre of Machine Learning for Social Good (ML4SG). Her research focuses on developing dependable and adaptive AI, spanning multimodal foundation models, representation learning, continual learning, domain generalisation and machine unlearning. She is particularly interested in how real-world scientific and societal challenges can drive fundamental advances in artificial intelligence, with applications in biodiversity conservation, environmental intelligence, and climate modelling. She has authored over 180 peer-reviewed publications and received multiple international research awards, including at ACM Multimedia, IEEE ICDE, AJCAI and IEEE DSAA. She has held numerous leadership roles in the international AI and machine learning community, including General Chair of IEEE ICDM and Co-Chair of the AI for Social Good Special Track at IJCAI–ECAI 2026.

Talk title

Token Perpetual Motion Machines and the Mystery of the KV Cache

Abstract

Large language models are machines that turn tokens into more tokens, endlessly feeding their own output back as input. In this talk we open up the machine. We follow the path from tokens and auto-regressive generation to the transformer and scaled dot-product attention, with a few historical detours along the way. This architecture at scale is what turned next-token prediction into AI systems with remarkable reasoning abilities.

We then turn our attention to the key-value (KV) cache, where tokens live in working memory. Since the cache grows with every token processed, it's size limits how much a model can read, think and remember. We survey the main strategies for squeezing more into this memory: summarisation, sliding windows, and sparse and linear attention, as well as eviction, merging and compression.

Finally, we present three pieces of our own work. The first evicts cache entries by counter-causal surprise, keeping tokens that the future cannot explain. The second presents a principled approach to merging entries by choosing a surrogate distribution, which gives closed-form corrections for token clusters. The third learns a compact cache that can be elastically expanded and compressed for both text and images.

Biography

Stephen Gould is a Professor of Computer Science at the Australian National University (ANU) and Chief Scientist at Metacognition. He is internationally recognised for his contributions to computer vision and machine learning, particularly the semantic, dynamic and geometric understanding of images and video, with advances in structured probabilistic models, declarative networks and large-scale scene understanding. Stephen has been an ARC Future Fellow, Director of the ARC Centre of Excellence in Robotic Vision, and Principal Research Scientist and Amazon Scholar at Amazon. He co-founded Sensory Networks, later acquired by Intel. He holds degrees in mathematics, computer science and electrical engineering from the University of Sydney, and a master’s and PhD from Stanford University. He has authored over 100 peer-reviewed publications and works closely with industry to translate research into real-world impact.

Talk title

AI for AI: thinking fast and slow from model to grid

Abstract

How much useful work can an AI factory deliver for each joule it draws from the grid? This keynote examines that question at two scales: the software that runs on a GPU and the power and cooling systems that support a fleet. In one case study, engineers used AI coding agents to optimise GPU kernels. A language model reached the same training quality target in 24% less time than the baseline on the same eight GPUs. At the factory scale, the talk considers how agents investigate faults and how they might anticipate cooling needs. Before a workload moves to another rack, for example, an agent could recommend adjusting coolant flow while local controllers retain control of the equipment. The distinction between fast and slow thinking runs through both examples: reasoning takes time, but a response that engineers have tested can be reused quickly.

Biography

Dr Daniel Kearney is Chief Technology Officer at Firmus Technologies. Based in Singapore, he leads the Engineering and Technology teams that design the company’s AI factories and the software that runs them. Daniel’s technology focus is building intelligent cyber-physical systems that help AI scale responsibly by matching computing workloads to the power the grid can supply and the heat a facility can remove. His work began at the scale of a single chip, with doctoral research at the University of Limerick on direct-to-chip liquid cooling for high-power processors. At ABB in Switzerland, his research evolved to integrated power electronics and MV systems before he switched to software at AWS, where he served as ASEAN Head of Technology. Daniel holds an MBA from IMD and is a Fellow of the American Society of Mechanical Engineers, where he serves on the Board of Governors.

Talk title

When is an AI Answer a Good Answer? Lessons from Language Technology and User Modelling

Abstract

Large language models have transformed AI's ability to generate fluent, context-sensitive responses. Yet a fundamental question remains: what makes an answer good? Is correctness enough, or must answers also be relevant, understandable, useful, and appropriate to the user's goals and circumstances?

This talk argues that a good answer is one that is fit for purpose. Drawing on more than four decades of research in language technology, dialogue systems, and user modelling, I revisit insights that predate modern generative AI but remain central to its future. These include adapting content to users, exploiting context, managing dialogue, and supporting personalised interactions. I then extend the discussion beyond answers to AI systems as sociotechnical systems, where trust, workflow integration, evaluation, and human-AI collaboration are critical. Ultimately, the challenge is not simply to build more capable AI systems, but to build systems that can be genuinely useful.

Biography

Dr Cécile Paris is a Chief Research Scientist at CSIRO, Australia’s national science agency and an Honorary Professor at Macquarie University in Sydney.  Cécile has 40 years of experience in research and research management in areas related to Natural Language Processing, User Modelling, Human Computer Interaction and, more generally, Humans and Machines.  Cécile was the Director of the recently completed Collaborative Intelligence program, a multidisciplinary research program developing the science to help people and machines work better together (and with humans empowered, rather than replaced, by AI). Cécile is a Fellow of the Australian Academy of Technology, Science and Engineering (ATSE) and of the Royal Society of NSW, the oldest learned society in the Southern Hemisphere.  Cécile received the ACS Australasian Artificial Intelligence Award for Distinguished Research Contribution in 2025 and the CSIRO Lifetime Achievement Award in 2024.  She has a bachelor’s degree from the University of California in Berkeley and a PhD from Columbia University in New York.

Talk title

Hyperspectral image analysis for computer vision

Abstract

Hyperspectral imaging captures dozens to hundreds of narrow, contiguous spectral bands, producing imagery rich in information about the spectral and spatial distribution of materials in a scene. Traditional hyperspectral remote sensing methods have focused mainly on pixel-level spectral analysis, often treating each pixel independently of its spatial context. Computer vision, in contrast, has made extensive use of color, texture, and other spatial and structural features of objects, but has largely overlooked spectral information. Bridging the gap between spectral and spatial analysis is therefore essential to developing new tools for effective image analysis and understanding. This talk provides an overview of hyperspectral imaging technology and three key analysis techniques. The first is spectral unmixing, which identifies the materials within mixed pixels and estimates their proportions. The second is band selection, which reduces data dimensionality while preserving discriminative information. The third is material-based spectral-spatial analysis, which characterizes objects by both their material composition and their spatial structure. It also shows how these techniques address challenges in computer vision tasks such as object detection, image classification, and video tracking, and presents several real-world applications that demonstrate their practical impact.

Biography

Jun Zhou joined the School of Information and Electronic Engineering at East China Normal University, Shanghai, China, in September 2026. Previously, he was a Professor in the School of Information and Communication Technology at Griffith University, a Research Fellow in the Research School of Computer Science at the Australian National University, and a Researcher at NICTA's Canberra Research Laboratory, all in Australia. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Alberta, Edmonton, Canada, in 2006. His research interests include pattern recognition, computer vision, and hyperspectral image processing, with applications in remote sensing, environmental informatics, and health. He is an IEEE Fellow and an Associate Editor of IEEE Transactions on Geoscience and Remote Sensing, IEEE Transactions on Multimedia, and Pattern Recognition.

Talk title

Efficient Strategies for Agentic AI

Abstract

Agentic AI is rapidly advancing toward increasingly complex reasoning and decision-making, but often at substantial computational cost. In this talk, I will present efficient strategies for agentic AI from various perspectives. I will discuss how computation can be dynamically allocated, redundant model capacity removed, and unnecessary reasoning avoided, with the broader goal of making agentic AI faster, leaner, and more scalable without compromising its capabilities.

Biography

Xinchao Wang is currently an associate professor in the Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering (ECE), National University of Singapore (NUS). His research interests include artificial intelligence, machine learning, computer vision, image processing, and natural language processing. He regularly serves as an area chair of major AI conferences, and as an associate editor of IEEE Transactions on Image Processing. He received the best paper award from VCIP 2022, best paper candidate from NeurIPS 2022, and IEEE Singal Processing Society Young Author Best Paper Award 2023. He is a recipient of Singapore President’s Young Scientist Award, IEEE AI’s 10 to Watch, and NUS Young Researcher Award.
