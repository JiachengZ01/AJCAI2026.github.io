Featured Talks and Abstracts

15:30–15:45

From Multimodal Models to Human-Centered Agents

Ting Dang

Abstract

Artificial intelligence has made remarkable progress through large-scale datasets and increasingly capable foundation models, yet building AI systems that reliably operate in the real world remains challenging. Real-world environments generate signals that are noisy, ambiguous, heterogeneous, multimodal, and incomplete. Speech may be unclear; physiological signals may be sparse or corrupted; and the meaning of a signal often depends on context. Multimodal foundation models provide new opportunities to learn from such rich signals, but important challenges remain in representing, reasoning over, and adapting to information distributed across modalities and time. These challenges become even more fundamental when AI moves from models that interpret inputs to agents that act with people in the world. My research asks: How can AI learn from complex and imperfect multimodal signals to build reliable understanding of people and their environments, and use that evolving understanding to interact and act effectively in the real world? My long-term vision is to develop human-centered AI systems and agents that can support people through interaction and action. My research sits at the intersection of machine learning, multimodal learning and applications in digital health and wellbeing.

About the speaker

Dr Ting Dang is a Senior Lecturer in Computing and Information Systems at the University of Melbourne, where she leads the Human-Centred AI for Multimodal Sensing (AIMS) Lab. Her research focuses on human-centred AI, speech and audio processing, multimodal sensing and adaptive AI with applications in healthcare, accessibility, and human–AI interaction. She previously held research positions at Nokia Bell Labs, the University of Cambridge, and UNSW, where she received her PhD. She is an IEEE Senior Member, an elected member of the IEEE Speech and Language Technical Committee, and an Associate Editor for IEEE Transactions on Affective Computing, Computer Speech and Language, and IEEE Pervasive Computing. Her research has received multiple best paper and outstanding paper awards at leading international conferences and attracted media coverage from outlets such as the BBC and The Guardian. She was also a finalist for the Women in Engineering Rising Star Award in 2025 and 2022.

15:45–16:00

Efficient On-Device LLM Agents: Compact, Adaptive and Grounded in Everyday Signals

Hong Jia

Abstract

Large language model (LLM) agents are moving onto the phones and wearables that people carry every day, where they can keep sensitive data local and offer timely, personal assistance, particularly in healthcare. This talk presents my research programme on efficient on-device LLM agents, spanning compact models, test-time adaptation and scaling, and everyday health sensing, and argues that AI capability can advance not only through scale but through better use of computation at inference time.

About the speaker

Hong Jia is a Lecturer at the University of Auckland and an Honorary Senior Fellow at the University of Melbourne. Before joining Auckland, Jia received a Ph.D. in Computer Science from UNSW and was a Research Associate at the University of Cambridge. Jia’s research spans edge AI, digital health, and human-centred AI, focusing on efficient and on-device LLMs, test-time adaptation and scaling, and multimodal AI with smart sensing to understand human behaviour, affect, and health.

16:00–16:15

Selective Structure for Trustworthy Language Models

Zhuang Li

Abstract

Traditional task-oriented dialogue systems mapped requests to executable representations. Domain-specific schemas and annotated examples limited performance with scarce data and made adaptation expensive. Frontier large language models (LLMs) can instead interpret requests and generate answers or actions directly. This reduces task-specific engineering but weakens inspectability: claims can lack evidence, and actions can violate rules outside the model. The design question is how to divide responsibility between the task LLM and external checkers. This talk examines when selectively introduced structured representations make outputs and actions more auditable without restoring semantic parsing’s data and schema bottlenecks.

About the speaker

Dr Zhuang Li is a Lecturer at RMIT University, specialising in semantic parsing and trustworthy large language models. He received his PhD from Monash University in 2023. His research explores how structured representations support reliable language generation and evaluation. Li was senior author of an EMNLP 2025 Outstanding Paper and received an EMNLP 2026 Outstanding Senior Area Chair Award.

16:15–16:30

Adapt, Align, Collaborate: Towards Safe Multilingual Foundation Models and Agents

Trang Vu

Abstract

Foundation models are becoming agents that act, collaborate and keep learning after deployment, for users who speak thousands of languages. This talk explores how foundation models and agents can adapt efficiently, remain aligned across languages and cultures, and collaborate reliably. These challenges are interconnected: acquiring new capabilities can weaken existing alignment, preferences can conflict across languages, and agent interactions can amplify individual errors. Drawing on my research in data-efficient learning, multilingual preference alignment, and model evaluation, the talk examines how diverse and sometimes conflicting learning signals shape model behaviour. It concludes with emerging challenges and research directions for maintaining reliability across model updates, linguistic contexts, and multi-agent interactions.

About the speaker

Trang Vu is a Lecturer in Data Science and Artificial Intelligence at Monash University. Her research focuses on building foundation models and AI agents that adapt efficiently, align with human values, and collaborate reliably across languages and cultures. She earned her PhD from Monash in 2022 and received the Vice-Chancellor’s Commendation for Thesis Excellence. She has published at top NLP/ML conferences, delivered an EMNLP 2025 tutorial, and served on the AACL-IJCNLP 2026 organising committee.

16:30–16:45

Reliable AI in an Open World: Generalisation, Adaptation and Beyond

Zijian Wang

Abstract

People routinely draw on what they have learned to make sense of unfamiliar situations. AI models, by contrast, can become unreliable when they encounter environments or tasks that differ from those seen during training. This gap motivates my central research question: how can AI generalise beyond its training conditions, and how can we determine and improve its suitability for new tasks?

My vision is to develop models that transfer effectively across environments and maintain reliable use of visual evidence during reasoning. This talk connects three strands of my research: domain generalisation and adaptation, transferability estimation, and adaptation of multimodal reasoning through post-training. Together, they address how to learn transferable representations, select suitable models and adaptation strategies, and improve model behaviour when existing capabilities are insufficient. I will conclude with emerging directions in reliable spatiotemporal reasoning.

About the speaker

Zijian Wang is a Lecturer at the School of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science, The University of Queensland. He is the recipient of ARC Early Career Industry Fellowship and leads industry-linked research with partners. His research focuses on model generalisation in multimodal AI and computer vision, with applications in reliable perception and real-world AI deployment. His work has been published in leading conferences and journals, including NeurIPS, CVPR, ICCV, ACM Multimedia, and IEEE TPAMI. He also contributes to the research community as an Area Chair for ACM Multimedia, Strategic Program Chair for Australasian Database Conference 2025, and program committee member and reviewer for prestigious international venues.

16:45–17:00

Learning within Limits: Safe Learning-Based Control for Energy and Robotic Systems

Meng Yuan

Abstract

Many engineering systems must achieve their performance goals within physical and safety limits. A fast-charging strategy should shorten charging time without accelerating battery degradation. A home energy management system should reduce operating costs while charging an electric vehicle to its target state of charge before departure. An assistive robot should track its desired speed while keeping its motion within safe bounds for its user. My earlier work enforced these limits with model predictive control (MPC), and my recent work learns control policies from data with constrained reinforcement learning. In this talk, I will present this research programme and outline future directions that combine the adaptivity of learning with the constraint guarantees of MPC.

About the speaker

Meng Yuan is a Lecturer in Mechatronics at Te Herenga Waka – Victoria University of Wellington and an IEEE Senior Member. He received his PhD in Control Systems from the University of Melbourne. He led the robotics control group at the Rehabilitation Research Institute of Singapore, Nanyang Technological University, and then an EU Marie Skłodowska-Curie project at Chalmers University of Technology. His research focuses on safe learning-based control and model predictive control for batteries, energy systems and robotics.
